Most software that handles documents asks you to upload them. The files go to a server, get processed there, and come back. That's convenient for the people building the software. For a law firm, it means client documents sitting on someone else's computer.

Execute doesn't work that way. Everything it does happens in your web browser, on your own machine.

## What stays on your computer

Final agreements are read in the browser. Signed pages and stamp papers are read there too, whether they're PDFs, scans or phone photos: text recognition runs on your computer, using a recognition file downloaded from app.agmt.legal. Matching, checks and assembly all happen locally, and the executed copies go straight to your downloads.

No document, file name or page text is sent to Agmt. Your signings are saved in your browser's own storage on that computer, so you can close the tab and pick up where you left off.

## Why that matters

Signature pages and stamp papers carry names, addresses, signatures and the shape of a deal. Keeping them off third-party servers is the simplest way to keep them confidential. There is nothing on our side to leak, lose or hand over, and the conversation with a firm's IT or risk team gets much shorter.

## No AI model, on purpose

Execute doesn't use an AI model to read your documents either. Small models that run in the browser do exist, but they're large downloads, they depend on the machine they run on, and they can invent names.

Explicit rules and text recognition are less magical and more dependable. Every match has a reason you can see, and every flag can be explained. When the output is an executed copy that someone will rely on, that matters more than cleverness.

## The trade-offs

Working this way has costs, and we'd rather say so plainly:

- A signing lives in one browser on one computer. There's no sync or sharing between colleagues yet.
- Clearing the browser's site data deletes your signings.
- Text recognition reads English, and doesn't read handwriting.
- Execute is tested in Chrome and Edge.

For documents like these, we think they're the right trade-offs.
