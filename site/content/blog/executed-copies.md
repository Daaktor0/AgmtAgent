When a multi-party agreement is signed, the signed agreement rarely exists as a single document. Each party signs its own signature page, often on a different day and in a different city, and sends it back: a scan from an office printer, a photo taken on a phone, a PDF, sometimes an original by courier. Where stamp duty applies, the stamp papers arrive too, sometimes with the page and sometimes days later.

The executed copy is what puts those pieces back together.

## What "complete" means

An executed copy is the final agreement with every signature page in place and signed. For each party, it is the document they will rely on: the one that goes into their records, gets shown to a bank or an auditor, and gets pulled out if the deal is ever questioned.

A complete executed copy has:

- the execution version of the agreement, exactly as it was signed off;
- every signature page, signed by the party it belongs to, in the right place;
- the stamp paper for that copy, where stamp duty applies; and
- nothing that belongs to a different document or a different party.

Each of those is easy on its own. The difficulty is that they arrive separately, out of order, and in every format at once.

## Where it goes wrong

The problems are rarely dramatic. A page comes back signed by the right person but from the previous draft. A stamp paper is bought in the wrong party's name. The same e-stamp certificate turns up on two parties' copies. One investor's page never arrives, and nobody notices until someone asks for the copies.

Every one of these is caught by careful reading. The trouble is that the careful reading happens at the end of a long deal, usually late in the day, across dozens of files with names like `IMG_2041.jpg`.

## Why every party needs its own

It can be tempting to circulate one bundle and let everyone take what they need. In practice, each party wants its own copy, with its own stamp paper in front and nothing missing, because that copy is the one it will file and rely on. Getting it right for eleven parties and wrong for the twelfth means the job isn't finished.
